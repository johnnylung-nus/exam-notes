Knowledge acquisition: elicitation versus discovery

Elicitation obtains knowledge from specialists; discovery learns patterns from recorded cases. Both concern how the system’s knowledge is built, rather than where the finished system runs.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixesExam answer

1. What problem does it solve?

Choose how to obtain eye-diagnosis and referral knowledge when specialist expertise is available but patient records may be incomplete or rare diagnoses poorly represented.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Useful expertise but few reliable patient casesPrioritize elicitation and validation with specialistsExpert knowledge can be formalized before a large labelled dataset exists.
Many representative, reliably diagnosed casesConsider knowledge discovery, with expert reviewData can reveal combinations that are difficult to enumerate manually, subject to label and coverage limits.

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • In elicitation, the information source is the specialist’s experience. Interviewing and checking cases exposes distinctions that have not already been captured in a large training table. The output could be rules, frames or another explicit representation; rules are not compulsory.
  • In discovery, the information source is historical evidence. The algorithm finds relationships between recorded observations and outcomes. Its conclusions depend on which cases were collected, how inputs and labels were recorded, and whether the training setup matches the future task.
  • The two routes can complement each other: mined patterns are candidates for specialist review, while expert knowledge can identify missing variables or implausible relationships. A pattern being frequent does not make it causally or clinically valid.
  • Acquisition happens when building or updating the knowledge. Runtime reasoning applies that knowledge to a current patient. A rule extracted yesterday and applied today is not an example of the deployed system learning during this consultation.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. List the knowledge needed: symptom/sign relationships, diagnostic distinctions, urgency and actions.
  2. For elicitation, interview specialists using concrete contrasting cases and ask them to explain important distinctions.
  3. Record the extracted knowledge explicitly and ask specialists to check exceptions and disagreements.
  4. For discovery, prepare historical inputs and verified diagnosis labels, check missingness, imbalance and chronology, then fit the relevant learned model or mine rules.
  5. Validate candidate knowledge on held-out cases and with specialists before use; maintain it when evidence or requirements change.

4. Design the solution and check the result

Data, components and application

  • Specialist time is limited → prioritize high-impact distinctions and record the rationale → reduces repeated interviews and makes checking possible.
  • Rare conditions are poorly represented → obtain suitable examples or define an explicit limitation/fallback → a model cannot learn reliable rare-case patterns from absent evidence.
  • Incorrect labels or future information → verify diagnoses and keep only inputs available at the decision time → avoids learning misleading relationships.

How to evaluate it

Check whether the acquired knowledge improves diagnosis and referral decisions on held-out cases, including rare conditions and important error types. Specialist validation checks meaning; held-out evaluation checks generalization. Neither alone proves perfect clinical performance.

Build the answer in this order

  1. Name both acquisition routes.
  2. Give one case-specific advantage and limitation for each.
  3. Tie claims to specialist expertise or patient-record quality.
  4. Keep runtime hosting and inference speed out of this acquisition comparison.

Common wrong answers and the exact correction
  • Experts do not guarantee correct or complete rules.
  • Discovery requires preparation and validation; it is not automatically faster or more accurate.
  • Do not replace the requested pros/cons comparison with “offline versus online” or “local versus cloud”.

6. Turn understanding into an exam answer

  • Elicitation can obtain explicit eye-diagnosis/referral knowledge directly from specialists without requiring a large labelled patient dataset, but extracting and checking that expertise takes specialist time.
  • Discovery can learn symptom/sign–diagnosis associations from historical cases, but rare eye conditions and unreliable records limit what it can learn.
  • I would validate either source of knowledge against held-out cases and specialist judgement; the acquisition method alone does not guarantee diagnostic accuracy.

7. Where this is tested in the sample papers

ISY5001 Q2(f) — Knowledge acquisition: elicitation versus discovery

What the question requires

  1. Compare how knowledge is acquired, not how it is deployed.
  2. Give one application-specific advantage and limitation for elicitation.
  3. Give one advantage and limitation for discovery.

Proposed answer points

  • Elicitation: specialists supply inspectable diagnosis/referral knowledge; obtaining and validating it takes scarce specialist time and tacit judgments may be hard to articulate.
  • Discovery: patient records can reveal symptom/sign associations; unreliable labels or too few rare-condition cases limit learning.

Avoid: Fast retrieval, offline use or centralised hosting do not distinguish elicitation from discovery.
