C046

Implicit feedback and preferences

Implicit feedback records actions such as views, clicks and purchases. It suggests interest but usually does not directly measure dislike or a rating level.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when users rarely provide explicit ratings but interaction logs are available.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Few explicit ratings but reliable behaviour logsImplicit recommendationUses available signals while retaining uncertainty
Need actual satisfaction predictionCollect satisfaction labels or validate a proxyClicks alone do not directly measure satisfaction

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Implicit feedback measures behaviour rather than a directly stated rating. A click, purchase or long view can indicate interest, but its interpretation depends on exposure and context. Buying a gift or accidentally clicking does not establish personal liking; no click may mean the user never saw the item.
  • Binary preference and interaction strength answer different questions. Mapping every observed event to one records evidence of engagement; weighting a purchase above a view introduces an assumption about strength. Predicting visit counts estimates activity intensity among observed behaviours, not automatically like-versus-dislike.
  • The absence of explicit negatives changes training design. Use an implicit-aware objective, controlled sampled comparisons or a ranking approach rather than blindly applying the explicit-rating interpretation. Evaluation should also match the event: predicting clicks may optimise curiosity while missing purchase or long-term satisfaction.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Define the event and what it plausibly indicates.
  2. Choose binary preference or justified event strength.
  3. Aggregate within a suitable time/session window.
  4. Choose an implicit-aware learning/ranking method.
  5. Evaluate against future relevant behaviour and practical outcomes.

C047

Jaccard similarity

Jaccard similarity compares two sets: number of shared elements divided by number in their union. It suits binary interaction sets without counting shared absences as agreement.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when each observed event means presence/interest and standard co-rated all-one vectors yield unhelpful identical similarities.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Binary positive interaction setsJaccard similarityNo need to invent numeric dislike ratings or count common absences
Explicit rating strength mattersUse a rating-aware comparisonSet conversion discards differences between one and five stars

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Jaccard compares sets of observed items rather than numeric rating levels. Its numerator counts common observed items and its denominator counts items observed by either user. Shared absences are excluded, so two inactive users do not appear highly similar merely because they both ignored a huge catalogue.
  • This addresses the lecture’s binary all-positive comparison problem. If pairwise-complete cosine considers only positions where both binary users have ones, their compared vectors can be identical and every pair scores one. Jaccard also uses observed items that are unique to either set, preserving differences.
  • Similarity is not automatically a rating predictor. With every observed value equal to one, a conventional weighted average of neighbour ratings still returns one. Use similarity to rank unseen items or another implicit-aware method, and check overlap strength; a perfect set similarity from one event is fragile evidence.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Create each user’s set of observed items.
  2. Find the intersection of the sets.
  3. Find their union.
  4. Divide intersection size by union size.
  5. Use the resulting similarity for neighbour/ranking logic, with overlap checks.

C048

Negative sampling

Negative sampling selects some unobserved user–item pairs as training negatives instead of treating the entire missing matrix as labelled dislike.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use for scalable implicit-feedback classification or ranking, while remembering that an unobserved item may actually be liked.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Huge implicit catalogue with sparse positivesSample negativesReduces computation while supplying comparison pressure
All negatives too easyTest realistic/popular/hard candidates carefullyEasy separation may not produce a useful top list

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Negative sampling converts a huge uncertain missing space into a manageable set of training comparisons or binary examples. Observed interactions are positives; some unobserved pairs are sampled as negatives. This is a modelling device, not evidence that the user explicitly disliked each sampled item.
  • The sampling distribution changes what the model learns. Uniform negatives may be easy obscure items, popularity-weighted negatives may be more realistically exposed, and hard negatives resemble positives and force finer discrimination. Hard negatives also risk selecting attractive items the user simply has not encountered.
  • Training and evaluation candidate policies must be documented. A recommender that separates positives from nine obviously irrelevant negatives can look excellent while failing to rank among thousands of plausible items. Excluding known positives from negative draws prevents direct label contradictions but cannot eliminate all false negatives in incomplete feedback.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Identify observed positive interactions.
  2. Choose a sampling policy and negative-to-positive ratio.
  3. Sample unseen candidates, excluding known positives.
  4. Train the classifier/ranker on positives and sampled negatives.
  5. Evaluate with a clearly stated candidate set and sampling policy.

4. Design the solution and check the result

Data, components and application

  • False negative → control/review sampling → lack of interaction may mean lack of exposure.
  • Biased easy negatives → use realistic candidates and compare policies → inflated evaluation may not reflect serving.

How to evaluate it

Check ranking/relevance and computational savings. Keep train/evaluation sampling transparent for fair model comparisons.

  2. Specify sampling.
  3. Describe learning target.
  4. Evaluate under realistic candidates.

  • Hardest negatives are not automatically best.

6. Turn understanding into an exam answer

  • “I keep observed clicks as positives and sample unobserved items, excluding known positives; sampled zeros remain uncertain negatives.”
  • “I compare sampling policies and evaluate realistic candidate rankings because easy negatives can exaggerate quality.”

C049

Confidence-weighted implicit MF

Confidence-weighted implicit MF separates preference from confidence. Any observed activity gives preference 1; no activity gives preference 0 with low confidence, rather than confirmed dislike.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when counts or durations provide stronger evidence of interest but are not explicit rating values.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Counts indicate strength but not explicit scoresConfidence-weighted implicit MFSeparate engagement presence from evidence weight
True explicit 1–5 ratingsObserved-rating modelDo not replace stated opinion with this binary interpretation unnecessarily

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Implicit MF separates the target preference from confidence in that target. The lecture maps any positive count to preference1 and no interaction to preference0. Confidence then weights how strongly the loss tries to match each preference, with repeated activity receiving more weight.
  • This does not convert every missing pair into an equally certain dislike. The unobserved preference0 carries low weight, while a repeated positive carries high weight. The factor model balances these weighted errors and regularisation, sharing patterns without claiming the binary preference matrix is an explicit rating survey.
  • Because unobserved entries still participate at low confidence, the objective differs from fitting only observed explicit ratings. An appropriate implicit ALS implementation exploits this structure; naively materialising every pair can be expensive. Confidence scale must be tuned: high activity does not imply proportionally increasing certainty forever.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Convert interactions to binary preferences.
  2. Assign higher confidence to stronger/more repeated observations.
  3. Retain low confidence for unobserved entries.
  4. Fit user/item factors with confidence-weighted preference error plus regularisation.
  5. Tune confidence scale/factors and rank candidates.

C050

Bayesian personalized ranking

Bayesian personalised ranking learns relative preferences: for one user, an observed positive item should score above a sampled unobserved item. It targets ordering rather than exact ratings.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Useful when implicit feedback supports a ranked list but no meaningful 1–5 target exists.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Implicit feedback and short ranked-list goalBPR-style pairwise trainingThe objective directly addresses relative ordering
Need interpretable numeric rating predictionUse suitable rating lossPairwise scores do not provide a calibrated star scale

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • BPR learns an ordering for each user rather than a direct numeric rating. An observed item should receive a higher score than a sampled unobserved alternative. The training example therefore contains a user and two items with an assumed preference direction, not merely one user–item label.
  • A pairwise loss penalises score differences that put the comparison item above the positive. Increasing the gap in the intended direction improves that comparison even if both raw scores move or have no 1–5 interpretation. Absolute score calibration is not the objective.
  • Materialising every possible item pair is expensive because the pair count grows quadratically with catalogue size. Sampling useful triples controls cost. The basic preference assumption remains imperfect: an unobserved item may be attractive but unseen. Validate ranked-list quality and the effect of negative sampling instead of claiming every non-observed item is disliked.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Select a user and an observed positive item.
  2. Choose an unobserved comparison item.
  3. Score both with the recommender.
  4. Update parameters to favour the positive item.
  5. Repeat with regularisation and validation, then rank eligible candidates.

Data, components and application

  • Unseen but attractive item sampled → acknowledge uncertain negatives → non-observation is imperfect evidence.
  • All item pairs materialised → sample training pairs → the possible pair count grows rapidly.

How to evaluate it

Evaluate held-out ranking/relevant-item retrieval rather than relying on rating MAE. Inspect negative-sampling sensitivity.

  1. Identify implicit ranking goal.
  2. Construct user–positive–comparison examples.
  3. Explain ordering objective.
  4. Validate rankings.

  • Pairwise preference is not a direct observed dislike label.
  • No need to invent a long formula to explain the mechanism.

C051

Hybrid recommenders

Hybrid recommenders combine complementary information or methods. Choose a combination that addresses a specific weakness, such as cold start or changing interests.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when neither content nor interaction history alone covers all users/items or objectives.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
New user has declared preferencesSwitch to contentUses actual information while CF is unsupported
Both methods reliable and complementaryValidated weighted fusionCombines evidence on a compatible scale

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Hybrid recommendation is justified by a failure that complementary evidence can repair. CF can use collective preference patterns but struggles with empty histories; content matching can score a described new item but may overspecialise. A design should explain which weakness each component addresses, rather than merely listing methods.
  • Combination patterns have different information flows. Switching chooses one method under a condition; weighted fusion merges compatible scores; mixed displays separate lists; cascade lets an earlier stage restrict candidates and a later stage order or break ties. Feature combination/augmentation passes information differently again.
  • Fusion has its own training and evaluation problem. Scores from two models may have incompatible scales, and a learned combiner must not train on overoptimistic in-sample component predictions. Compare the whole hybrid with each component, including cold-start coverage, diversity and serving cost, to show that complexity earns a useful improvement.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Identify each method’s strengths and failure cases.
  2. Choose a lecture pattern: weighted, switching, mixed, cascade, feature combination/augmentation or meta-level.
  3. Specify the information/scores passed.
  4. Tune combination parameters on validation data.
  5. Evaluate the full system and each component.

C052

Deep collaborative filtering

Deep collaborative filtering learns nonlinear user–item interactions. In neural CF, user and item embeddings feed an MLP instead of relying only on a dot product.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Consider when sufficient interaction data and features justify more capacity than simple MF.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Dot-product model underfits supported interactionsCompare MLP/NeuMFNonlinear combinations may capture additional structure
Very sparse data and no side featuresKeep regularised MF baselineDeep capacity may memorise rather than generalise

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Neural CF replaces or extends the simple dot-product interaction between user and item embeddings. An MLP can learn nonlinear combinations, but the diagram must show what enters the embeddings and what the output predicts. User/item identifiers select learned vectors; their raw numeric size is not a meaningful feature.
  • Deep architecture does not change the need to define labels. Explicit ratings suggest a regression loss; sampled implicit positives/negatives suggest a classification or ranking objective. An autoencoder is a different design: it compresses a rating/interactions vector and reconstructs outputs, so missing-value treatment must be specified rather than silently trained as dislike.
  • Additional capacity can learn useful feature interactions when enough evidence exists, but can also memorise sparse IDs. Side information can help with content and new entities only if the architecture actually consumes it. A deep ID-only model does not know a new item’s description merely because such descriptions exist elsewhere in the system.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Define user/item inputs and explicit or implicit target.
  2. Map IDs or features to embeddings.
  3. Combine embeddings with the chosen neural layers.
  4. Train with a loss matched to rating or binary/ranking target.
  5. Tune capacity/regularisation and compare against MF.

Check held-out recommendation quality, coverage, latency and model size. Separate candidate retrieval from expensive ranking if catalogue is large.

C053

Sequence-based recommendation

Sequence-based recommenders use the order of a user’s interactions to predict the next useful item or rank candidates for current intent.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Useful when recent session behaviour matters, such as a shopper’s changing goal or news interests.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Current anonymous browsing intent mattersSession-based recommenderUses available recent sequence without requiring lifelong profile
Catalogue is hugeRetrieve candidates then sequence-aware rankExpensive attention need not score every item online

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • A session sequence represents a changing intent through ordered events. A bag of viewed items loses which event happened recently and which preceded it. A next-item model learns from prefixes to a later target, while a candidate-ranking model uses the sequence to judge each supplied candidate; these are distinct outputs.
  • Different lecture models use sequence evidence differently. Prod2Vec learns item relationships from session context using a word-embedding analogy; GRU4Rec processes ordered events through recurrent state; attention/Transformer methods can weight relevant past events rather than letting the latest accidental click dominate.
  • Training examples must stop at the prediction time. A label such as the next purchase or click belongs after the prefix and cannot appear among its input features. Session boundaries, repeated events and timestamps need consistent handling. Recent evidence can be useful without proving that a user’s temporary browsing goal represents permanent taste.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Group and time-order events into user/session sequences.
  2. Encode items and available context.
  3. Train next-item prediction or candidate ranking using only earlier events.
  4. Use RNN/GRU or attention/Transformer according to the lecture design.
  5. Serve candidates/rankings and update session context.

  • Future leakage → chronological construction/split → prediction cannot use later clicks.

Evaluate next-item/ranking success on later events plus latency. Check anonymous sessions and short histories.

C054

Graph-based recommendation

Graph-based recommendation models users, items and other entities as nodes, with interactions/relationships as edges. Recommendation can become missing-link prediction.

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • A recommendation graph can combine user–item interactions with categories, creators or social links. That structure may connect entities with little direct rating overlap, but each edge type must retain its meaning: friendship, purchase and membership are not interchangeable levels of preference.
  • Graph representations can be learned through sampled walks or neighbour aggregation. Node2Vec turns walks into contexts for embedding learning; GraphSAGE samples and aggregates neighbour features. A link scorer then predicts whether a user–item connection is plausible. Explain this chain instead of calling the graph itself a recommender.
  • The graph must respect the prediction boundary. Removing the held-out edge from the loss is insufficient if that same edge remains available during embedding construction or neighbour messages. New-node behaviour also depends on available features and graph connections, not on the mere existence of a graph-neural label.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Define node and edge types.
  2. Construct a graph using training-time information only.
  3. Learn representations through random walks or neighbour aggregation.
  4. Score possible user–item links or retrieve nearby item embeddings.
  5. Filter seen/ineligible items and rank.

4. Design the solution and check the result

Data, components and application

  • Future/test edges included → remove them before training/features → topology can leak the answer.
  • Sparse/new nodes → use features/fallback → graph structure alone may provide little evidence.

How to evaluate it

Evaluate held-out link/recommendation rankings, cold-node behaviour, scalability and source quality.

Build the answer in this order

  1. Draw typed graph.
  2. Explain embedding/scoring.
  3. Prevent edge leakage.
  4. Justify extra relationship value.

Common wrong answers and the exact correction
  • A possible link is not an established fact.
  • Random walks and graph neural aggregation are different training mechanisms.

6. Turn understanding into an exam answer

  • “I represent users, items and sports with typed edges, learn representations from training-only links, then score possible user–item connections.”
  • “For B, shared sport and friend history are supporting signals, not verified preference; I remove target edges before embedding and evaluate held-out rankings.”
