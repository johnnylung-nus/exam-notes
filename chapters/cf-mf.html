Collaborative filtering & matrix factorization

What problem? → How it works → What can go wrong? → What to change and why?

Discussion overview retained; detailed lecture-linked tutorials and sample-paper applications follow below. Worked values are illustrative unless explicitly attributed.

1. What are CF and MF? What problem do they solve?

  • Problem: most users have rated or interacted with only a small part of the catalogue. Predict what each user may like and recommend useful unseen items.
  • Collaborative filtering (CF): learn from patterns across users and items. You do not need a written description of every item to use basic CF.
  • User-based CF: find users with similar rating patterns; use their feedback to make a recommendation. Item-based CF: find similarly rated items; use the user’s feedback on those items.
  • Matrix factorization (MF): a model-based method within CF. Learn a short list of numbers for each user and item; their match gives a predicted rating.
  • Relationship: CF is the recommendation approach; MF is one way to implement it. They are not two compulsory consecutive algorithms.

2. One small example: from ratings to a prediction

  • Illustrative example, not recovered from the earlier chapter chat. Movie ratings: A rated M1=5, M2=4, M3 unknown; B rated M1=5, M2=4, M3=5; C rated M1=1, M2=2, M3=1.
  • User-based reasoning: A’s known pattern resembles B’s more than C’s. B’s rating is evidence that M3 may suit A. It is a recommendation signal, not proof A will give 5.
  • MF reasoning: use all observed training ratings to learn user and movie factors. Factors describe patterns learned jointly; do not assume each one has a known label such as “action”.
  • Prediction calculation: suppose a trained model gives A’s factors [1, 2] and M3’s factors [2, 1]. Multiply matching positions and add: 1×2 + 2×1 = 4. This is a basic predicted rating, not a probability.
  • What “learn” means: the numbers above are supplied for calculation. In training, the model adjusts factor values so predictions better match known ratings; we do not manually assign these values.

3. MF steps: what happens, and why?

  • 1 — Prepare: keep user ID, item ID, feedback, and time if available. Remove erroneous records; decide how repeat interactions should be aggregated. Unknown ratings stay unknown.
  • 2 — Split: separate training, validation, and test interactions before fitting the model. Use earlier interactions to predict later ones when that matches the scenario.
  • 3 — Choose a starting k: k is how many factor values represent each user/item. Start with a manageable size; choose the final k using validation results.
  • 4 — Initialize and predict: start user/item factors with small values. For each known training rating, combine the user’s and item’s factors to predict it.
  • 5 — Calculate error and update: compare prediction with the actual rating; adjust factors to reduce the loss. With stochastic gradient descent (SGD), updates use individual training ratings or small batches. Repeat across the training data.
  • 6 — Validate and stop: check held-out performance, tune k and regularization, and keep the best validation model. Test once after choices are fixed.
  • 7 — Recommend: score eligible unseen items, order the scores, and recommend the highest-ranked items. A high predicted score is an estimate of preference, not a guarantee.

4. What is the loss? Explain it before writing symbols

  • Basic explicit-rating loss: add up squared differences between actual and predicted known training ratings. Squaring makes errors positive and puts greater weight on large mistakes.
  • Small calculation: actual ratings [5, 3], predictions [4, 4] give differences [1, −1]. Squared errors are [1, 1]; their total is 2. This shows prediction-error loss, not the complete regularized objective.
  • Regularized loss: prediction-error loss plus a penalty for large factor values. The penalty discourages fitting every accidental detail. Its strength is tuned on validation data.
  • Training loss vs evaluation: the loss guides updates on training data. A validation/test metric measures performance on held-out observations. Low training loss alone does not show good recommendations.
  • Implicit feedback: a click/watch is evidence of interaction, not an explicit rating. Do not blindly reuse an explicit-rating objective: the handling of unobserved pairs must match the task.

5. Data problems: problem → remedy → reason

  • Sparse matrix: most user–item pairs are missing. Similarity estimates may have little shared evidence; factor estimates may also be weak. MF shares patterns across ratings, but does not create information from nothing.
  • Practical response: store observed user–item–rating records rather than a huge dense table; use a small enough factor model and regularization. Sparse storage saves memory; it does not itself improve prediction quality.
  • Do not fill missing ratings with 0: that tells the model an unknown item was disliked. For explicit-rating MF, fit known ratings and predict the missing ones. Collect more relevant feedback where possible.
  • Few ratings vs no history: an existing user with a little history may still get an estimate from shared patterns. A completely new user/item has no learned factors from interactions: this is cold start.
  • Cold-start response: ask a new user for a few preferences; use a popularity/average fallback initially; or use item/user information with a content-based or hybrid approach. Explain that extra information is being added to basic CF.
  • Different rating habits: one user may rate everything generously. If this is a problem, account for user/item baseline tendencies so systematic generosity is not mistaken for special preference.
  • Clicks and repeated events: no click is not automatically dislike; the item may never have been shown. Keep the meaning of feedback clear and do not let accidental duplicates pretend to be independent evidence.

6. How do I evaluate it?

  • Split roles: training learns factors; validation chooses settings/stopping point; test estimates final performance. Do not choose k using test results.
  • Rating prediction: compare predicted ratings with held-out actual ratings. MAE is average absolute error; RMSE squares errors, averages them, then takes the square root. Lower is better; RMSE reacts more to large errors.
  • Calculation: errors [1, −1] give MAE=(1+1)/2=1; RMSE=sqrt((1+1)/2)=1, in rating units.
  • Top-item recommendation: ask whether relevant items appear near the top. Precision@N = relevant items among the N recommendations / N. Recall@N = relevant items retrieved / all held-out relevant items. State what counts as relevant.
  • Ranking calculation: recommend 5 items; 3 are relevant; the user has 6 relevant held-out items. Precision@5=3/5=0.60; Recall@5=3/6=0.50. Here N counts recommendations; k counts factors.
  • Check fairness of comparison: use the same split and candidate rules for competing models; compare against a simple average/popularity baseline. Evaluate new-user cases separately if those are the intended task.
  • Avoid leakage: do not train on future ratings and then claim to predict the past. Do not compute fitted rating averages or model parameters from held-out feedback.

7. Overfitting: recognize it and choose a remedy

  • Recognize: training error falls while validation error rises. The model is fitting training-specific detail rather than improving on unseen feedback.
  • Reduce k: fewer factor values mean less capacity to fit noise. Too few can underfit, so validate rather than assuming “smaller is always better”.
  • Add/increase regularization: penalize large factor values so a few observations cannot pull estimates too far. Too much can underfit; select the strength using validation.
  • Early stopping: stop training when validation performance stops improving and retain the best checkpoint. More training passes are not automatically better.
  • Improve useful evidence: collect genuine additional feedback and remove erroneous/duplicated records. Copying the same rows is not new information.
  • Example choosing k: k=[1,2,3,4,5]; training RMSE=[1.30,0.85,0.48,0.20,0.05]; validation RMSE=[1.40,1.00,0.80,1.20,1.50]. Choose k=3 here, not k=5. Values illustrate the decision; they are not lecture results.

8. Turn the scenario into an answer

  • Suggested MF, very sparse ratings: “MF is suitable for learning shared user/item patterns, but sparse feedback makes estimates uncertain. I would tune a smaller factor count and regularization on validation data; use a fallback when history is insufficient.”
  • Training improves, validation worsens: “This indicates overfitting. I would reduce model capacity or increase regularization, stop at the best validation point, and check the final model on untouched test data.”
  • Unknowns replaced by zero: “Missing is not the same as dislike. These zeros introduce false negative ratings. I would preserve missingness and use an objective appropriate for the observed feedback.”
  • Good RMSE, weak recommendation list: “Rating accuracy and ranking quality are different. I would evaluate relevant items in the top-N list and choose settings consistent with the recommendation objective.”
  • Answer order: identify the task → select/assess the model → identify the specific problem → propose the change → explain why → say how to evaluate the change.

C038

User-based collaborative filtering

User-based CF recommends what similar users like. Similarity comes from shared rating/interaction patterns; it is a neighbourhood-based form of CF.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixesExam answer

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when enough users have overlapping histories to make their similarities meaningful.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Substantial shared rating historiesUser-based CFNeighbour preferences can transfer useful evidence
Voluntary sparse patient feedbackReject unsupported user similarity or use justified fallbackLow overlap undermines the comparison mechanism

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • User-based CF asks which other users show a similar pattern of preferences, then borrows their opinions on items the target has not rated. Shared-item overlap is essential evidence: two people cannot be reliably called similar merely because both have many ratings somewhere in the catalogue.
  • Prediction weights each usable neighbour by similarity. For a candidate item, only neighbours who rated that item contribute. If user rating habits differ, Pearson/mean-centering compares relative liking rather than treating a generous 5 and a strict 4 as different tastes by default.
  • The sample treatment scenario requires reasoning through the mechanism: sparse voluntary feedback means few commonly rated treatments, which makes patient similarity unreliable, which weakens recommendations. Saying “not enough data” omits this causal chain. Also, the case rates treatment sets per consultation, so individual-treatment matrix cells cannot be counted without an explicit assumption.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Build user–item ratings with blanks retained as unknown.
  2. Compare the target user with others on valid shared items.
  3. Select similar users who rated each candidate item.
  4. Compute a similarity-weighted rating or preference score.
  5. Rank eligible unseen items and test held-out results.

C039

Item-based collaborative filtering

Item-based CF compares items using how users rated them, then recommends items related to those the target user liked. It remains collaborative filtering.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Useful when item similarities can be precomputed and remain more stable than user similarities.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Many users, relatively stable item interactionsPrecomputed item-based CFMove substantial similarity work offline
Rapid new catalogue with no item historiesContent/hybrid fallbackPrecomputation cannot create missing interactions

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Item-based CF represents each item by its pattern of user interactions, then compares items across users. It is not content matching: two films with different descriptions can be similar because the same users rate them similarly. Transposing the rating matrix changes the comparison perspective, not the source of evidence.
  • For a target user and candidate X, find items that user has rated and that are similar to X. Their ratings, weighted by item similarity, determine the candidate score. Different users therefore receive different predictions even though the item-similarity table is shared.
  • Precomputation can reduce serving work because item relationships often change less frequently than individual interests. The advantage depends on user/item counts and catalogue change, not an absolute law that item-based is always faster. New or rarely interacted items still lack collaborative evidence and may need content or popularity fallback.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Transpose the interaction perspective: each item is described by user ratings.
  2. Compute item similarities using suitable overlap/normalisation.
  3. Precompute a neighbour list for each item.
  4. Use the target user’s ratings of similar items to score candidates.
  5. Exclude seen/ineligible items and rank.

C040

Similarity and rating prediction

Similarity defines what counts as a useful neighbour. Prediction then combines neighbours’ known ratings, often with similarity weights and rating-scale adjustment.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when a scenario asks how ratings are predicted or why a similarity choice is misleading.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Users use different rating baselinesPearson/mean-centeringRelative likes/dislikes can align despite generosity differences
Document profiles differ mainly in lengthCosine often appropriateDirection matters more than raw count magnitude

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • A similarity measure is meaningful only after specifying the coordinates and missing-value policy. Cosine compares direction, Euclidean measures absolute separation, and Pearson centres rating patterns to reduce differences in users’ rating baselines. These answer different notions of resemblance.
  • The prediction equation must match the chosen normalisation. If ratings were centred around user means, combining centred deviations gives a deviation that must be converted back to the target scale. Mixing raw scores with a centred-model interpretation produces values with the wrong meaning.
  • A numeric similarity can hide weak support or undefined arithmetic. Zero variance makes Pearson problematic; no overlap means no pairwise-complete comparison; a zero usable-weight sum prevents the weighted average. These cases require an explicit fallback or abstention rather than quietly fabricating a confident score.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Choose comparison coordinates and missing-value policy.
  2. Use cosine for direction, Euclidean for distance, or Pearson for centred rating patterns.
  3. Require sufficient relevant overlap.
  4. Select neighbours with known candidate ratings.
  5. Compute the stated weighted prediction, restoring a mean if centred.

3. Illustrative similarity versus rating-style bias

Worked study example: Users rate the same three films. Strict S gives [2,3,4], generous G gives [3,4,5]. Numbers are invented to isolate baseline effects.

Data, components and application

  • Zero-filled unknowns → use a justified missing-data policy → absence is not a measured zero.
  • Tiny overlap/zero denominator → fallback or abstain → a numeric score may be unreliable.

How to evaluate it

Compare held-out predictions and coverage under alternative similarity/normalisation policies.

C041

Recommendation evaluation

Evaluation must match the goal: rating error checks score prediction; top-list metrics check recommendation usefulness. A low rating error does not ensure a useful ranked list.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixesExam answer

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when comparing recommenders, choosing factor count or assessing readiness to deploy.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Predict an explicit numeric ratingMAE/RMSECompare with held-out known values
Show a short recommendation listPrecision/recall at K plus rank-sensitive check if neededEvaluate the user-visible selection

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Rating prediction and recommendation ranking are related but different objectives. MAE/RMSE compare numeric estimates with known held-out ratings. Precision/recall at K assess which relevant items appear in a displayed list. A model can predict middling ratings accurately yet miss the few useful items that should reach the top.
  • The evaluation candidate set and unknown labels must be explicit. If only known test ratings are evaluated, the result measures performance on that restricted set; it does not reveal whether every unseen catalogue item is irrelevant. The lecture workshop also allows fewer than K qualifying recommendations, so record the actual denominator used.
  • Precision@K ignores positions within the selected set. If order is important, a rank-sensitive measure is needed in addition. Beyond accuracy, coverage, diversity, new-user performance and serving cost determine whether a recommender solves the real scenario. Select settings on validation and reserve final test data.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Define relevant items and what the user actually sees.
  2. Hold out known interactions/ratings without leaking them into training.
  3. Tune on validation data and reserve a final test.
  4. Calculate MAE/RMSE for rating predictions, precision/recall at K for relevant lists.
  5. Also assess coverage, diversity and operational cost.

C042

Sparsity and cold start

Sparsity means most user–item pairs lack observations. Cold start means a new user/item has little or no history. They weaken different parts of recommendation.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixesExam answer

1. What problem does it solve?

Use to assess whether neighbourhood CF or interaction-only MF has enough evidence to personalise.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
New user with zero historyAsk starter preferences/use declared fallbackNo interaction method can infer a supported personalised vector from nothing
Existing users have scattered overlapping historyConsider validated MF/hybridShared structure may help, but cannot guarantee coverage

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Sparsity is not just a large number of blanks: it determines whether the chosen mechanism has enough evidence. User-based CF needs common ratings to compare users; item-based CF needs common users to compare items; MF shares latent patterns but still needs observations to learn each entity’s factors.
  • Cold start is the extreme case of no useful history for a new entity. More latent dimensions or a different similarity formula cannot estimate a brand-new user’s tastes from no evidence. Starter preferences, content attributes or an explicit nonpersonalised fallback supply a different information source.
  • The meaning of one observed entry must be established before computing density. In sample Q3(c), a patient rates a treatment set at a consultation, not necessarily every individual treatment. Converting set feedback into several individual ratings is an assumption that changes the data and may introduce misleading labels.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Identify users, items and what one feedback entry actually rates.
  2. Estimate observed density only with justified counts.
  3. Check user/item history and shared overlap.
  4. Use content, onboarding or popularity fallback where appropriate.
  5. Collect feedback and validate transitions to personalised methods.

C043

Matrix factorization

MF is a model-based CF method: it learns a short hidden-factor vector for each user and item from interaction ratings. Their dot product predicts a rating.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use to share patterns across many sparse ratings without calculating a neighbourhood for every prediction.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Many sparse ratings with shared preference patternsRegularised MFA small factor model can share statistical structure
Brand-new item with zero interactionsContent/hybrid fallbackIts item vector cannot be reliably learned from no ratings

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • MF is a model-based method within collaborative filtering. It learns compact user and item vectors from ratings rather than explicitly searching similar users at every request. A coordinate is a learned latent factor; assigning it a genre name is optional interpretation, not something the algorithm knows from ratings alone.
  • The dot product is the prediction mechanism: multiply corresponding user and item entries and add them. Training adjusts these entries so known ratings are approximated while regularisation limits unnecessary magnitude. There are usually far fewer factors than catalogue items, allowing shared patterns to connect sparse observations.
  • For explicit ratings, blank cells remain unknown rather than becoming target zeroes. The training objective is assessed on observed training ratings, and held-out ratings must not influence fitting. Ordinary SVD of a completed numeric matrix is not automatically the same procedure; keep the separate SVD/PCA teaching problem distinct.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Prepare observed user, item, rating records and split them.
  2. Choose latent-factor count and initialise vectors.
  3. Fit known training ratings with a regularisation penalty.
  4. Tune factor count/penalty on validation data.
  5. Score unseen items by user–item dot product and rank eligible ones.

C044

SGD / ALS training

SGD and ALS are two ways to learn MF user/item factors. SGD adjusts them from rating errors; ALS alternates which side is fixed while solving the other.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when asked how factors are trained or why factorisation is an iterative optimisation rather than manually assigning genres.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Streaming through observed triplesSGDLocal updates can process rating examples incrementally
Conditional least-squares solves/parallel side updates suitableALSFixing one side decomposes fitting into many independent subproblems

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • SGD updates the factors involved in a single observed rating using the direction of its error. If prediction is below the actual value, the update should increase the compatibility for that pair while regularisation restrains factor growth. Other pairs sharing that user/item are indirectly affected, explaining how training couples observations.
  • ALS turns the joint problem into alternating conditional problems. With user factors fixed, item factors can be fitted by least squares; with items fixed, users can be fitted. The overall task remains iterative because improving one side changes the best choice for the other.
  • Both procedures require the same clearly defined target and evidence policy. Explicit-rating missingness and implicit preference confidence produce different losses, so “use ALS” alone is not a complete data design. A stable training-error decrease is also insufficient: tune on validation and stop before overfitting or budget limits.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Choose factor count, objective and regularisation.
  2. Initialise factors.
  3. SGD: predict a known rating, calculate error and make a small update to the involved vectors.
  4. ALS: fix user vectors and solve item vectors, then reverse.
  5. Repeat while checking validation performance; stop appropriately.

C045

Recommendation overfitting and regularization

Overfitting means learning training-specific noise instead of patterns that transfer. In MF, too many factors or large weights can fit sparse observations too closely.

How it worksWorked exampleChoose & designProblems & fixes

1. What problem does it solve?

Use when training error falls but validation worsens, or a proposed recommender is too complex for available evidence.

Scenario cueSuitable choiceWhy it fits
Training improves while validation worsensReduce capacity/raise penalty/stop earlierConstrain fitting of training-specific noise
Both training and validation poorCheck underfit, features and data qualityMore regularisation can worsen insufficient fit

2. How it works — what happens and why

  • Overfitting is diagnosed by transfer, not by model size alone. More factors usually allow lower training error, but sparse ratings leave many parameter combinations weakly constrained. The model can fit accidental observations while making unstable predictions for unknown items.
  • Regularisation changes the optimisation objective by penalising unnecessarily large factor weights. This discourages an extreme fit to a few ratings, but a penalty that is too strong can flatten genuine preferences. Factor count, penalty and training duration interact, so compare them through validation rather than prescribing a universal K.
  • Early stopping uses validation behaviour over training time: retain the model before held-out performance worsens even if training loss continues falling. This is distinct from simply stopping when training converges. A final test should not be reused to choose the best epoch or factor count.
Implementation steps and their purpose
  1. Separate training, validation and test data.
  2. Compare training and validation error as complexity/training increases.
  3. Tune factor count on validation.
  4. Add regularisation to discourage excessively large factor weights.
  5. Stop before validation degrades and evaluate once on final test.
